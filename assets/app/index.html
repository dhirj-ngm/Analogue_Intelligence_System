How this works

What a match means

Search connects a small set of input names in your description with pressures in company reports. It links milk with dairy, flour with grains, chocolate with cocoa, and freight with transport. It also recognizes coffee, cattle/livestock, aluminum/aluminium, copra (dried coconut kernel), paper and cardboard. Each result shows the words it connected. Related inputs are not identical costs or proof of a useful comparison.

Simple sentence rules separate input costs from sales or demand, keep denied pressures out, and compare whether costs rose or fell. For example, “milk costs are rising not falling” searches for a rise. “Milk costs fell, energy bills rose” keeps each change with its input. We set aside situations about businesses outside this collection, such as airlines. A supplier's or customer's industry is considered separately from your business. When the wording is unclear, we may have no comparison to show. A list of input topics can still be searched, with its direction marked as unspecified.

The rules cannot read every sentence or recognize every industry. Check what we read from your description and what we left out. Some phrases still fall outside the vocabulary: beef is not automatically treated as cattle, and PET needs a material word such as resin or plastic. Live-animal wording prompts you to clarify what you buy. We do not assume it means cattle. If directions conflict or we cannot tell whose costs changed, we ask you to edit the description. The edit button keeps your words and dates; it does not submit another search. Related terms and matching directions do not show that an earlier action would work now.

Each case lists the pressures it supports and those not established here. Missing evidence does not mean those costs did not change. Counts cover the named pressures we could identify; complex descriptions can still be misread. Combining partial cases does not document one combined situation.

Fuel, petrochemical materials, and natural or cooking oils are kept separate. Bare “oil” prompts a clarification. Copra is a dried kernel, not coconut oil; paper/cardboard evidence does not establish pulp. A named-input match means both descriptions identify the material, with grade and purchase basis still unknown. A related comparison can name a narrower input or only a broader cost connection. Oil-related distribution costs may offer related fuel context, but they do not establish diesel or another exact fuel. Results and comparisons keep that limit visible.

Country names do not filter or boost search results. Check each case's location and business scope.

Where the evidence comes from

The case descriptions were compiled with AI from published company reports. They have not had a complete independent review. Each case links to its report and tells you where to look. The company's explanation is not independent proof of cause and effect.

Dates, repeated cases and later events

We check publication dates, not just the dates a report covers. When you start from a case, we exclude it and known continuing reports. Repeated copies are grouped together. Reported actions and later outcomes do not help a case rank higher. Actions not known by your chosen date are hidden.

Search method and this search's checks

BM25 scores named input families in the pressure passages only. Responses, profit figures and later outcomes do not affect these scores. General words such as ingredient, commodity, cost or margin are not enough. Clauses are checked for role, direction, negation and business context before matching. Material names receive more weight than related material links. Length checks count actual pressure mentions, rather than the number of possible links. Scores are keyword scores, not confidence or probabilities. This vocabulary and its simple negation rule do not establish relevance.
