## What the case study should help a reader investigate

The intended case connects a pressure to company exposure, reported effects,
feasible responses, stated reasoning and dated subsequent observations. A reader
needs to understand why a comparison helps their question and what limits it.
It is insufficient to find two reports that both say costs rose and margins fell.
Cross-company history can reveal different exposures or responses. Own-company
history can reveal changes in category mix, contracts or commercial flexibility;
the same company name does not establish stable conditions.

Accepted direction comes from D001–D005 and D008 in
[DECISIONS.md](../../DECISIONS.md). The mechanisms below are teaching models and
investigation prompts. Whether a particular relationship holds in a real case
requires its own evidence and attribution.

## An accounting model before a business explanation

For positive net revenue R, gross profit is R minus cost of goods sold (COGS).
Gross margin is gross profit divided by R. Operating profit also deducts the
applicable operating expenses; its margin uses the same revenue denominator.
Interest and taxes usually enter further down the income statement. Inspect
each issuer's presentation and accounting definitions before comparison.
The [SEC financial-statement guide](https://www.sec.gov/investor/pubs/begfinstmtguide.htm)
explains these stages; they do not identify the economic causes of a change.

A deliberately simplified, single-product model is:

```text
R = p × q
COGS = v × q + F
gross profit = (p − v) × q − F
gross margin = 1 − v/p − F/(p × q)
operating profit = gross profit − O
operating margin = operating profit / R
```

Here p is the realized net price per standardized unit, q is units sold, v is
variable cost per unit, F is fixed manufacturing cost recognized in COGS, and
O is other operating expense. Assume production equals sales, no inventory
change, consistent cost recognition, one product/currency and unchanged F/O.
Real disclosures need not reveal these variables. This model excludes product
mix, currency, acquisitions, inventory accounting and many capacity effects.
It is a conditional accounting identity, not an estimated causal model.

Holding the other variables fixed, larger v reduces gross margin; larger p
increases it; larger q spreads F over more units. If F is zero, volume alone
does not change gross margin in this model, although it changes gross profit.
In practice p, q and costs can change together. The partial effects do not
predict the total effect of a pricing decision.

## Hypothetical numerical illustration — all values invented

Currency values use arbitrary monetary units. F = 100 and O = 100 throughout.
Each row is a separate scenario, not a claimed company sequence or forecast.

| Scenario | p | q | v | Revenue | COGS | Gross profit | Gross margin | Operating profit |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Starting situation | 10 | 100 | 6 | 1,000 | 700 | 300 | 30.00% | 200 |
| Higher unit cost | 10 | 100 | 7 | 1,000 | 800 | 200 | 20.00% | 100 |
| Price rises; volume unchanged | 11 | 100 | 7 | 1,100 | 800 | 300 | 27.27% | 200 |
| Price rises; volume is lower | 11 | 95 | 7 | 1,045 | 765 | 280 | 26.79% | 180 |
| Same lower volume; unit cost reduced | 11 | 95 | 6.5 | 1,045 | 717.5 | 327.5 | 31.34% | 227.5 |

The third row restores the original gross-profit amount, while gross-margin
percentage remains lower. The fourth has revenue 4.5% above the starting value
and units 5% below it: sales growth does not establish volume growth. Fixed cost
per unit rises from 1 to approximately 1.053 when q falls from 100 to 95.
The last row assumes a productivity improvement reduces v; it does not estimate
what any real programme would deliver or its investment cost.

A movement from 30% to 20% is a fall of 10 percentage points, or 1,000 basis
points. It is not a fall of 10 percent relative to the starting margin.
Changing the profit amount and changing its revenue share are different questions.

## How the business concepts connect

| Concept | Mechanism to investigate | What the case should show | Assumption or limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Input cost pressure | Materials, packaging, energy or freight become more expensive for the firm | Exposed inputs, reported cost change, scope and timing | A market commodity index does not establish the firm's purchase price or recognized cost |
| Margins | Revenue, recognized costs and expenses change relative to one another | Gross versus operating margin; level/change; source-defined drivers | A declining margin alone does not distinguish cost, demand, mix or exceptional expense |
| Demand and volume | Customers' willingness to buy, shipments, availability and channel inventories interact | Company's volume definition, demand commentary and stock/availability context | Shipments are not automatically consumer sell-through; falling units need not mean weaker underlying demand |
| Pricing | Net realized prices change through list prices, discounts, promotions or pack changes | Announced versus implemented change, realization and timing | A list-price announcement need not translate into the same net price; a price/mix disclosure may not separate them |
| Productivity | Less input, waste, time or expense is needed for comparable output | Programme, reported savings basis, implementation and associated costs | Reported savings can mean avoided future cost; they need not equal a fall in total expense |
| Constraints | Contracts, competition, affordability, capacity, cash or qualifications limit response | Source-backed constraint, affected action and relevant period | An action not reported is not proof the company could not take it |

### Exposure and timing

A material price rise matters in proportion to exposure and when it reaches
recognized expense. Procurement contracts, hedges, inventory and currency are
possible reasons for different timing, not inferred facts about a firm.
Compare an external shock with a firm's disclosed exposure rather than treating
every FMCG company as having the same input basket. Freight may be presented
in different expense categories across issuers; check classification.

### Price, volume and mix

For one unchanged product, revenue growth is exactly
`(1 + price growth) × (1 + volume growth) − 1`. Adding the two growth rates is
only an approximation. In a multi-product business, `R = Σ p_i q_i`; a shift
toward expensive products can raise average revenue per unit without any
individual price increase. Use issuer-defined price/volume/mix bridges with
their scope and rounding notes. Do not manufacture an exact decomposition.

Price elasticity describes a proportional quantity response to a proportional
price change, conditional on a specified demand relationship and other factors.
Two reported quarters do not identify it: competition, availability, consumer
income, promotions and inventory may also have changed. Pricing power here is
an investigation concept concerning feasible price realization and trade-offs,
not a label proven by a revenue increase or an unreported pricing action.

### Responses and constraints

Possible responses include net pricing changes, pack adjustments, waste reduction,
procurement changes, product reformulation or operating redesign. These are
discussion possibilities, not required labels or company recommendations.
Supplier changes may require product qualification; a retailer agreement may
delay price realization; a smaller pack may lower cash outlay while raising price
per unit of contents. Each proposition requires case evidence before use.

For example, in an invented case management states a rationale of preserving
access to an entry-price pack and reports implementing a smaller pack. Preserve
that statement as the company's stated rationale. An analyst may interpret the
action as prioritizing affordability over pack size; that interpretation needs
its own label and may be challenged. Neither is a universal principle.

### A primary-source terminology check, outside pilot curation

Kimberly-Clark's 2021 Form 10-K, MD&A “Analysis of Results of Operations,”
separately discusses volume, input costs, net selling prices and cost savings.

supports preserving distinct driver categories and issuer attribution. It does
not provide an independently identified effect for each response.

## From these concepts to a readable output

Present the challenge and dated conditions first, then source-supported driver
relationships with their attribution. Explain the relevant match and differences.
Show documented actions, stated reasoning and later observations with dates.
Keep absent implementation, rationale or follow-up evidence visibly unknown.
For the first experiment, later response/outcome content stays outside similarity
features under D003; presence in the case display does not permit ranking use.

If margin later improves, candidate explanations include realized pricing,
changed input costs, volume/mix, expense changes and accounting differences.
The chronology “response, then recovery” does not establish how performance
would have evolved without the response. A management driver bridge is an
attributed explanation, not that missing counterfactual. Cases support learning
about reported trade-offs and evidence; they do not prove which strategy works.
